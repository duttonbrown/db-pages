Is your production-automation work pointed at the right goal?

A full audit of db-operations against one question: how close are you to "production runs itself" — and the 10 highest-value moves to get there.

The one-line answer

Yes — your direction is right. You're building the right thing. But you've over-invested in features and under-invested in closing loops and adoption.

The custom production stack (Shopify → Notion spine → travelers → wash list → consoles) is the correct architecture for a made-to-order, color-batch-across-orders shop. Six independent deep-dive agents agreed on this, and on the same root problem: the machine is mostly built, but it doesn't run by itself and the floor isn't using it yet.

Two facts tell the whole story, pulled live from Notion today:

You have built a system that creates production data beautifully and advances it almost not at all. Every stage transition — Powder Coat → Assembly → QC → Shipping → Complete — is a human hand-editing a Notion field, and right now those humans aren't doing it. That's not a code problem. That's a close-the-loop + adoption problem, and it's where the next 10 moves should concentrate.

The build-vs-buy question, settled

Stay custom. The MRPeasy migration is effectively already dead — and that's the right call.

The research's own make-or-break test concluded MRPeasy cannot do the wash list natively (aggregate powder-coat parts by color across orders) — it's an architectural mismatch, not a config gap. Every honest version of the buy plan ends in "…and then write the Python scripts anyway," while paying $300–900/mo and adding manual MO labor on the floor. Your color-batch-across-orders reality is something every packaged MRP fights and your custom stack fits.

Keep one door open: the single place custom is genuinely weak is part-level inventory with a real transaction ledger (raw → coated → finished, decremented on batch-complete) plus purchasing/receiving and QuickBooks COGS. Don't buy for it yet — prove you can build it (Task 9). Only if that drifts against a physical count does a narrow back-office tool (likely Odoo, not MRPeasy) earn its keep.

The 10 highest-value moves, ranked

Ordered by leverage toward "production runs itself." The first three close the loop and prove the system; the rest harden, de-risk, and clean up. #1–3 are the ones that change the trajectory.

  1. Auto-complete on shipment — close the loop with a fulfillment webhook

    New · smallCloses the loop

    Register a Shopify orders/fulfilled (or ShipStation) webhook → n8n → match order # → set Production Log Location = Complete + stamp Shipped At. Zero floor action required — it fires from the shipping desk event using data Shopify already has and you currently throw away.

    Why #1: It's the only break in an otherwise-closed loop, it needs no human, and every piece already exists (webhook harness, listener bridge, Notion patch, order-# dedupe key). It's a few-hours build that turns the spine from a write-once log into a self-maintaining status board — the prerequisite for trusting it to drive anything. Today 0/419 rows ever get marked shipped.

  2. Auto-advance Powder Coat → Assembly on batch complete

    New · smallCloses the loop

    When the wash-list server completes a batch, write back to every included Production Log row: advance Location Powder Coat → Assembly and stamp + Wash List / Powder Coat ETA. Also fix the hole where custom-color batches can't advance the spine at all (spine_advance.py).

    Why #2: 377 of 419 rows are frozen at Powder Coat — this is the single highest-volume manual edit in the whole system, every order passes through it, and the wash server already knows exactly which rows belong to which batch. Closing it advances ~85% of the open queue automatically.

  3. Ship the Lead Time console — it's one wire from removing you

    Finish · 3 stepsRemoves a person

    Wire the leadtime/* tunnel route + Task, publish the page to /production/lead-times/, and delete the Hextom step from the runbook. Add a try/finally so a partial apply always logs a revertable record.

    Why #3: It's the only console blocked by mechanics, not people, and the only one that removes a human (you) from a recurring loop the moment it ships. Highest certainty-to-value ratio on the list — finish what's done.

  4. Make a Notion write-failure impossible to miss

    Finish · smallTrust / adoption

    Every console deliberately lets Notion writes fail silently (right call for floor continuity — the floor falls back to hand-editing). But there's no reconciliation and no alert, so a failed write looks identical to "working." Add a Teams Adaptive Card on any notion=FAILED (the routing pattern already exists).

    Why: Silent desync is the fastest way to lose floor trust on day one. Small change, directly protects adoption of everything above.

  5. Schedule + guard the BOM rebuild (the production brain)

    New · smallDe-risk core

    Add a scheduled rebuild of extract_bom.py plus a staleness guard so the wash list warns or refuses when running on an out-of-date BOM, and a dropped-row log so unresolvable parts stop vanishing silently. Fix DB-RefreshPartsLibrary in the same pass (it's currently failing, exit 1).

    Why: The BOM is the spine of the entire production stack yet it's the only link with no auto-refresh and no freshness check. Every "automated" downstream decision silently inherits its staleness.

  6. Solve triage + re-split so the spine matches how the floor really works

    New · mediumAdoption keystone

    Build post-triage re-split (spine self-relation + a Manual Split flag the hourly sync respects). Resolve the latent bug where the console patches rows the sync re-derives — today they can fight and clobber floor work.

    Why: The floor won't triage because triage can't model rod/bulb assignment + splitting. This is the load-bearing unbuilt piece — until the spine is trustworthy, every downstream console reads partly-stale data. Higher effort, which is why it sits below the quick loop-closers, but it's the keystone for real adoption.

  7. Run the first real floor cut — drive adoption, don't add features

    Adoption · you-ledHighest ROI on built work

    Sit with Scottie/Willy and get the first real wash-list cut and first real triage pass through the console — not a test order. Watch where it fights them; that list is your real backlog. Then one assembler on real timers.

    Why: Every agent independently flagged the same thing — ~zero organic floor use across all consoles. More features compound the gap. The build is largely done; the missing ingredient is one supervised real run that converts "built" into "used." This is yours to drive, not Claude's to build.

  8. Consolidate & un-break the monthly KPI pipeline

    Finish · mediumVisibility

    It's failing silently (exit 4, no logs) and duplicated — a Windows orchestrator and a GitHub Action both write the same Notion Snapshots DB on overlapping schedules. Collapse to one path (prefer GitHub Actions — a sleeping PC can't miss it), add run logging + a Teams failure alert, and replace the hardcoded QuickBooks income table (frozen at 2026-04) with a live Intuit pull.

    Why: Production KPIs are how you'll know automation is working. Right now the scoreboard quietly lies as the calendar advances.

  9. Prove part-level inventory before you ever consider buying an MRP

    New · spikeStrategic de-risk

    30-day spike: take the top ~50 highest-usage parts, stand up a part-stock ledger fed by the BOM extractor + batch-complete events (raw → coated → finished, decremented automatically), and test it against a physical count. Wire in the Parts Request → Inventory Request handoff (31/34 floor requests are stuck at "Submitted," oldest from March).

    Why: This is the one capability the research says only a packaged MRP gives you. If your ledger holds against a real count, the entire buy case collapses and you've saved $300–900/mo forever. If it drifts, you've scoped the exact narrow slice worth buying.

  10. Pay down the structural & security debt that taxes every change

    Structure · mediumMaintainability
    • Rotate the leaked Notion key — a live key is committed in fetch_all_parts.sh + ~12 other tracked files. Rotate, purge, replace with the .env read. Do this first.
    • One shared client lib — there are 15 copies of the Notion client and 3 of the Shopify client. A 429-retry fix today means editing a dozen files. Add common/ and collapse them.
    • Promote the consoles out of projects/ and document them — your core automation is buried 4 levels deep and CLAUDE.md doesn't mention it, the hub, or db-private at all.
    • Archive the dead weight — ~13 parked folders, resolve the data/production vs projects/data/production shadow.

    Why last: None of it blocks today, but it raises the cost of every move above. The key rotation is the one genuine risk — do that part immediately even though the section ranks #10.

How to read this list

#1, #2, #3 are the trajectory-changers. Do them first and in order — they're small, they need no floor behavior change, and together they make the spine self-maintaining for every transition that doesn't require human judgment (powder-coat return, shipment). That's the proof-of-life that makes the harder, people-dependent stages (#6, #7) worth pushing.

#7 is the one only you can do. Everything else Claude can build. Adoption is a CEO-led act: one real cut, watched, with the floor. The single biggest risk to this entire investment isn't a missing feature — it's that a fully-built system sits unused because nobody walked the floor through their first real run.

The strategy is sound. You are not reinventing an MRP poorly — you're building the one production system that actually fits a color-batch, made-to-order shop, and packaged tools can't. The job now is to finish the loops and earn the adoption, not to build more surface.
